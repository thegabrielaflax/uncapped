# The offer bank: what someone could get paid for

Gabriela's generation menu (10 November 2026). Use it to widen what you consider for every person, never as a checklist or a mechanical mapping. The person should see a curated set based on their evidence, interests, capacity and access to buyers.

## Start from the person
Start with the problems they know how to solve, the decisions people trust them to make, and the things they've learned at work and outside it. Then use the bank to ask: who else might pay for that skill, when would they need it, and what would they buy?

Surprise is welcome when it's grounded: a new buyer, a different industry, a problem they've never thought to charge for, or a larger piece of work they already know how to deliver. Look beyond the industry they work in for buyers with a similar problem.

## Before you write the garden
- Go through every family in the bank and ask whether their evidence supports an idea there. Most people with years of professional experience have a real idea in advisory, in agency or partner work, and in writing that brings in leads. Include each one their evidence supports.
- Look beyond the industry they work in: include at least two ideas whose buyers are outside it but share the same problem.
- Include the bigger piece of work they could already deliver: a project or sprint sized to most of their available hours, priced like the senior work it is. Don't price a seasoned specialist's project as if it were cheap hourly work.
- A hobby or passion gets one card in a first garden. Its variations (a corporate version, a kit, a bigger event) go in that card's next step.

## A worked example (an invented person, to show the range)
Sam has spent nine years as a logistics coordinator at an online furniture retailer, fixing the delivery promises customers don't trust. Sam loves running pub quizzes, has about four hours a week, wants an extra $400 a month to start, and never wants to manage a warehouse. A range that fits Sam:
- A believable first sale: a 90-minute delivery-promise review for a small online shop, about $300, one session.
- The bigger piece of work: a delivery experience sprint, about $2,000 as a hypothesis, 8 to 10 hours across a month. Sam reviews the checkout's delivery promises, the carrier setup and the complaints, then hands over a prioritized 30-day fix plan on one handoff call.
- Buyers beyond furniture with the same problem: a meal-kit company, a plant nursery shipping live plants, a print-on-demand shop.
- Agency or partner work: a Shopify agency pays Sam to review the delivery setup across client stores before launch.
- Advisory: a founder whose operations assistant runs fulfillment pays Sam for a monthly review.
- A digital product: a $15 delivery-promise checklist a small shop can use before a sale weekend.
- Writing: a newsletter about why online orders go wrong, which brings in consulting leads first, with paid analysis or sponsorship later if a readership builds.
- The passion, once: a ticketed pub quiz night. A corporate quiz or a quiz pack would be its next step, not extra cards.
Sam's goal of $400 a month could come from one or two reviews a month, or one sprint every few months: two very different weeks, and the garden shows both.

## Describe each offer so they can picture it
The category tells them the type. The offer itself has to tell them what they'd sell. For every offer, answer concretely:
- What would I do? What they'd write, create, teach, review, package, deliver or build, described as the actual work, so they can picture opening their laptop and knowing where to start.
- What would the buyer get? What they'd open, attend, receive or walk away with. If the format is hard to picture, a quick example of what's inside or how they'd use it.
- What changes for them? One punchy sentence about what they can do, decide or fix afterward, said the way you'd say it to a buyer, and never bigger than the offer can deliver.
- Who could buy it, and when? Recognizable client types and a reason they'd look for it now. If a host or employer would pay and someone else would use it, say which is which.
- Why does this fit me? A relevant story, skill or example from Discover, kept apart from the guesses about buyers, pricing or demand.
- What's the first test? A small action with a plausible buyer, and what it would tell them: someone trying a draft tells you something different from someone paying.

By type: a guide says what's inside, with an example of a page or section, and when someone would reach for it. A workshop says what they'd teach, what participants would practice in the session and what they'd leave able to do. A packaged resource says which files or materials they'd create, what's in them and how someone uses them alone. A service says what they'd review or do, what they'd hand over and what the client could do with it next. A tool says what the buyer puts in, what comes out and what that helps them decide.

Sam's checklist, written this way (an invented example):
- Format: Downloadable checklist. Title: Before-the-sale delivery checklist for small online shops.
- What changes: Spot the moment your checkout promises Tuesday and your carrier says Friday, before a customer complains.
- What you'd make: a one-page checklist a shop owner uses with their checkout and carrier settings open side by side, checking that the promised date, the cutoff time, the shipping price and the tracking email match. Include a worked example of a sale weekend where the cutoff was wrong, and two ways to fix it.
- What they'd get: a checklist they reuse before every sale or new product, plus the worked example.
- Who might buy it: a small online shop owner running their own sales; a founder launching a first holiday sale; a solo operations assistant at a small brand. When: a week before a sale or a new product launch.
- How to test it: show a one-page draft to a few shop owners with a sale coming up and ask them to use it, then offer the finished version at a set price to see whether anyone pays.

## Curate, don't fill
- Each idea must be commercially distinct. Changing a call into a PDF only earns another card if it meaningfully changes who buys it or why.
- One idea per pattern is enough in a first garden (one tasting idea, not three). Use the space to show other opportunities.
- Keep what they're excited about, even if it isn't the easiest to sell.

## Income goal and price are separate
- Their income goal tells you what would make a first experiment worthwhile. It never sets the price.
- Price from their experience, the buyer's problem and the scope of the work. Someone who wants an extra $500 a month might get there through one $500 engagement, a $2,000 project every few months, or an entirely different model. Show the trade-offs.

## Scope to their capacity
- Respect what shapes what they can deliver: their hours, their evenings, their firm noes.
- Show what they could deliver with most of their available time, as well as what they could try in a single session.
- Where an offer would need more capacity, a larger audience or stronger proof than they have, say so plainly.

## Make the offers easy to compare
For each idea: who pays; what happens that makes them look for help; what they get; why this person could credibly deliver it (grounded in something they shared); a suggested test price; the total time it takes to deliver; and one practical way to test whether someone would buy. Label the buyer, price and demand as guesses to test wherever they're untested.

Across the garden, show which offer is easiest to test, which could bring in more per client, and which they seem most excited to try. Those can be three different ideas. Where there's a plausible next step, show it: a one-off strategy sprint might lead to recurring advisory, a workshop to a toolkit or a license, a newsletter might bring in buyers before it earns directly.

## Rules to check
Employment contracts, licenses and professional rules are things to check, never facts you state. If someone may be restricted from outside work (a contract, a non-compete, a conflict with their employer's competitors), flag it plainly on the offers it affects and make the first step conditional on what they find.
