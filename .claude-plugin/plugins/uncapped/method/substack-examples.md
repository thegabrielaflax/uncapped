# Worked example: Catherine (the level every strategy copies)

Catherine is the fictional test customer: a senior growth marketer at a snack brand who's testing a 90-minute ad-and-page session for small online food and wellness brands, with a free 20-minute call called Before You Boost Another Post. Everything marked *(hers)* comes from her saved answers. Her nutrition certificate stays out of this publication, because it's for a different reader (method 1b).

Copy the level of detail, the voice and the depth of research, never the content: every customer's posts come from their own answers. Catherine is fictional.

### Her publication

**Who you're writing for.** Picture the founder of a small granola or snack brand who boosted a post for $100 last week, watched it reach a few hundred people, then opened Shopify and couldn't say whether a single order came from it. She's about to spend again because a launch is coming, and she hasn't asked anyone for help because she assumes everyone else can read their ad numbers.

**The belief you'll repeat.** Check what happened before you spend more, and remember that not knowing is fine as long as you fix it next time *(your voice answer)*.

**Your named thing: the Couch Test.** Before you pay for another ad, watch your own the way a stranger would, scrolling on your couch, then tap through and try to buy. You already do this *(what you know)*. It gets named in post 3 and comes back in posts 4, 5 and 9.

**Your series: Did That $100 Do Anything?** Now and then, one founder tells you about her last boost and you read it together, in public, with her permission. Post 8 is the first one.

**Why she subscribes before she's ready to buy.** Every issue gives her one thing to check before she spends again, from someone who sounds like a friend leaning over the table rather than an agency.

### Full card: post 3

#### The Couch Test: watch your ad like a stranger before you pay for another one
*Five minutes, your phone, and the question your dashboard can't answer*
**Methodology · The named thing · Ready**

**Who she is.** Picture the founder of a small granola brand who's been running the same Instagram video for three weeks. People click on it, a few leave nice comments, and yesterday she messaged a creator to ask for a new video because the orders haven't followed. She opens Ads Manager most mornings and knows her cost per click to the cent. In all that time, she has never once scrolled to her own ad in her own feed, tapped it, and tried to buy, because it hasn't occurred to her that a stranger's experience might be completely different from the one in her dashboard.

**What she's stuck on.** She's decided the video is the problem, so she's about to spend another few hundred dollars replacing it. She doesn't actually know where people are leaving. It could be the moment the page asks for a box when the ad showed a single bag, the moment the price appears, or the last screen, where shipping doubles the cost of trying something new. Without looking, she'll keep fixing the part she can see and keep paying for clicks that leak out somewhere she has never been.

**Where your offer comes in.** This post gives away the first fifteen minutes of what you'd do in a session, which is to look at the ad and the page together through a stranger's eyes. Most readers who try it will find more than one thing that feels off. That's exactly where a second pair of eyes is worth paying for, because choosing the one thing to change first takes judgment you've built over eight years in Ads Manager and they haven't.

**Here's the idea.** I love this one because it takes something you already do without thinking and turns it into a named habit your readers can adopt tonight. You told me you notice food ads everywhere now, even scrolling on your couch, and that at work you're the person who re-watches the video and clicks through on your phone. Most founders don't have that instinct, because they only ever see their ad as a row of numbers. The post names it the Couch Test, walks the reader through doing it on her own ad, and shows her what she's likely to find. The name is the point. Once readers start asking each other "have you done the Couch Test?", your publication has a phrase that belongs to you, the way Anna Mackenzie owns Shoot Your Shot Wednesday.

**How the post unfolds.**

*Beat 1, the couch.* Open on you on a Sunday evening, scrolling past a snack ad from a brand you've never heard of and tapping through before you've decided to. Let the reader catch you getting all the way to checkout just to see what shipping costs, and admit that your partner has stopped asking what you're doing. This is where the reader decides she likes you, so keep it light and specific.

*Beat 2, the gap between the dashboard and the feed.* Turn to her. She has probably only ever met her ad inside Ads Manager, where it's a row of clicks and costs, while a stranger meets it wedged between a friend's holiday photos and a dog video, with a few seconds to decide whether it's for her. Name the habit here, the Couch Test, and say in one sentence why it works: the dashboard tells you how many people tapped, but not what they found when they did.

*Beat 3, the steps.* Hand over a numbered list she can follow tonight:
1. Find your ad in your own feed rather than in Ads Manager.
2. Write down in one sentence what it promises.
3. Tap through.
4. Find the product, the price and the pack size.
5. Try to check out, stopping at the first moment you'd hesitate if you'd never heard of the brand.

Give each step a sentence on what to notice, so it reads like you're sitting next to her.

*Beat 4, what she'll probably find.* Show three common gaps in a few sentences each: the ad sells one snack and the page asks for a box; the ad shows the crunch and the page shows a flat-lay photo; the shipping cost only appears on the last screen. Bring in your own flavour ad in two lines. A video about trying one flavour sent people to a page selling twelve bags of it. That's proof this happens even at brands with a full marketing team, and readers who saw post 1 will smile at the callback.

*Beat 5, pick one.* End on restraint. She'll probably find more than one problem, and the mistake is fixing them all at once. Use your own lesson: you changed the video and the page together and still can't say which one did the work. Then give her a sentence to finish: "The first place a stranger would hesitate is ___." That sentence is her homework and the bridge to the ask.

**Bring the world in.**
- **The Pepsi Challenge.** Pepsi launched it in 1975 as a blind taste test: shoppers tasted two unlabelled colas and picked one. The Couch Test is the same idea for an ad, so take away the dashboard and see what a stranger actually experiences. One line comparing them gives your habit a bit of history. [Pepsi Challenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepsi_Challenge)
- **"Half the money I spend on advertising is wasted."** The line is usually credited to the retailer John Wanamaker, though Quote Investigator found its history is messier than the legend. It's a good setup for why you look at the page before you blame the video, and the disputed origin is a fun aside. [Quote Investigator](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2022/04/11/advertising/)

**Read before you write.**
- **Anna Mackenzie, "No one cares so take bigger swings".** It shows how she introduces Shoot Your Shot Wednesday as a little weekly ritual with exact steps. Borrow the way she makes a habit feel personal and easy to copy. [Read it](https://annamackstack.substack.com/p/no-one-cares-so-take-bigger-swings)
- **Ellie Frazier, "The algorithm doesn't hate you, your content is just kind of boring".** She takes a weak hook and rewrites it in front of the reader. Borrow that before-and-after rhythm for beat 4. [Read it](https://elliewfrazier.substack.com/p/the-algorithm-doesnt-hate-you-your)

**What you're drawing on.** Noticing ads on your couch *(what you know)*. Clicking through on your phone, and the twelve bags *(your work story)*. Changing the video and page at the same time *(your work story)*.

**Before you write.** Check your employment contract before mentioning your brand's ad, and keep the brand unnamed. Do the Couch Test on two or three real small-brand ads first, so your examples in beat 4 come from things you actually saw. Describe them by type rather than naming the brands.

**How it ends.** Shape B, "Your next step".
- **Bridge:** "If you did the Couch Test and found three things and no idea which to fix first, that's completely normal, and it's the part I can help with."
- **Wording:**

> **Your next step**
>
> "I did the Couch Test and found three things and no idea which to fix first."
>
> That's what my free 20-minute call is for. It's called Before You Boost Another Post. Bring the ad and the page, share your screen or send screenshots, and we'll pick the one worth checking before you spend again, even if you've never opened Ads Manager. [add your booking link]

Then the standing footer.

### Full card: post 6

#### I made six adults taste crackers blind. The $9 one lost.
*What people choose when they can't see the packaging, and what that means for your ad*
**Audience builder · The playful experiment · Do this first**

**Who she is.** Picture the founder of a small cracker or snack brand who spent months on her packaging (the colours, the illustration, the little joke on the back) and loves it. She's also the founder most likely to forward a funny newsletter to her co-founder and her founders' group chat. This post is written for her and for everyone she sends it to, which is why it's the most shareable post in your set.

**What she's stuck on.** She believes people buy her snack because it's delicious and the bag is beautiful, and in a shop, where someone can pick it up, both may be true. Online, a stranger meets it as a few seconds of video in a feed, without the weight of the bag in her hand or any chance to taste it. That means the ad has to do the job the packaging and the flavour do in a shop. Most founders never stop to think about what their snack is like when everything they're proud of is taken away.

**Where your offer comes in.** The tasting is a joke with a serious question underneath, and that question is exactly what you look at in a session: what makes a stranger want to try a snack when all she has to go on is a few seconds of video? This post doesn't push. It makes readers like you and remember how you think, and the footer does the rest.

**Here's the idea.** This is the post people forward, and it's yours because you told me you've always wanted to run a snack detective club. Six adults taste grocery-store snacks blind, with little scorecards, everyone taking it far too seriously. Run it once with the friends who'd come and write it up. It's funny on its own, and underneath it asks the question at the centre of your work: once the packaging, the price and the brand are gone, which snack do people actually reach for again, and what does that tell a founder about the few seconds her ad gets in someone's feed? Lauren Sudeyko does this brilliantly with Sleep or Die. Her playful ideas always connect back to the product, and they're why people stay subscribed between the practical posts.

**How the post unfolds.**

*Beat 1, the setup.* Set the scene with as much real detail as you can: who came, where you held it, what you bought and where from, the price range from the gas-station bag to the $9 one, and the scorecards you made. Include the two friends who already have strong opinions about which grocery store makes the best hummus, because readers love a cast.

*Beat 2, the tasting.* Describe the moment you realise six adults are arguing about a cracker, what people wrote on their scorecards, and which snack won. Hold the price reveal until the end of the beat, because that's the payoff. If the $9 bag lost, say so. If it won, the headline changes and that's fine. Use what really happened.

*Beat 3, what surprised you.* Without the bag, people judged on crunch, saltiness and whether they wanted a second one, and the things the brands had spent money on never got a vote. This is where the wine study fits (see below). When researchers dyed a white wine red, tasters described it in red-wine language, which shows how much what we see shapes what we think we taste.

*Beat 4, your ad is a blind tasting.* Turn to the reader. Online, a stranger meets her snack as a few seconds of video without tasting it or holding the bag, so the ad has to show what the taste and the packaging would have told her in a shop. Ask her to watch her current ad with the sound off and say out loud what a stranger learns about how it tastes. This links back to the Couch Test from post 3.

*Beat 5, the question to take away.* Close by asking her to name the one thing a stranger should notice first about her snack, and to check whether it appears in the first three seconds of her ad. Then invite readers to suggest a snack for the next tasting, which keeps the club going as a series.

**Bring the world in.**
- **The Pepsi Challenge.** Pepsi started setting up blind taste tests against Coca-Cola in 1975, and it became the most famous blind tasting in snack history. A line comparing your living-room table to Pepsi's shopping-mall table gives the post a wink. [Pepsi Challenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepsi_Challenge)
- **The wine-dye study.** In 2001, researchers at the University of Bordeaux gave tasters a white wine dyed red, and they described it as if it were red. It's the perfect evidence for beat 3, that what we see changes what we taste. [The original paper](https://linguist62n.stanford.edu/morrot01colorofodors.pdf)
- **Little Moons.** In January 2021, a few simple TikTok videos of people buying and eating Little Moons mochi went viral, and Tesco reported a 700% jump in sales. There was no dance or trick, just someone eating the product. Use it in beat 4 as proof that showing the eating can sell. [The Grocer](https://www.thegrocer.co.uk/news/mochi-tiktok-craze-sees-little-moons-sales-rocket-700-at-tesco/652665.article)

**Read before you write.**
- **Lauren Sudeyko, "Cassandra Grey Falls Asleep in 9 Minutes".** This is the first episode of her playful interview series. Borrow the quickfire section and the closing "who should we do next?" comment prompt, which turns a fun idea into something readers come back for. [Read it](https://laurensudeyko.substack.com/p/cassandra-grey-falls-asleep-in-9)
- **Snaxshot, by Andrea Hernández.** This food and beverage newsletter is known for funny, slightly snarky cultural observations about snack brands. Read a couple of issues for tone, and for how humour and real industry knowledge sit together. [Adweek on Snaxshot](https://www.adweek.com/media/substack-snaxshot-food-and-beverage/)

**What you're drawing on.** The snack detective club *(something you'd love to try)*. Wanting more in-person things *(more of this)*. The friends with hummus opinions *(who you know)*.

**Before you write.** Run the tasting. Photograph the scorecards, write down the funniest lines word for word, and ask your friends whether they're happy to be named. Describe the snacks that lost by price or type, never by brand name.

**How it ends.** Shape D, the soft door.
- **Bridge:** "If you've never watched your own ad as if you'd never tasted the snack, that's the next tasting I'd run."
- **Wording:**

> Reply with the snack you'd bring to the next one, and I'll put the best suggestion on the table. And if you have a friend who'd argue about a cracker, send her this.

Then the standing footer.

### The brief for post 3

**Here's the idea.** This is the post where your couch habit gets a name, and readers leave able to do it tonight.

**Headline options.**
1. The Couch Test: watch your ad like a stranger before you pay for another one *(my pick, because it names the thing and makes the promise)*
2. Your ad looks different from your couch
3. Before you brief another video, try to buy your own snack

**Two ways in, and my pick.** You could open on yourself, on the couch on a Sunday, catching yourself auditing a stranger's granola ad, or on her, about to brief a creator for a new video without ever having tapped through her own ad. I'd open on you. It's funny and true, and it makes the reader want to know what you see. Bring her in by the third paragraph.

**How I'd open it.** You on the couch, phone in hand, scrolling past a snack ad and tapping through without meaning to. The detail that makes it is that you've reached the checkout of a brand you've never heard of, just to see how much shipping costs. Lines you could borrow *(suggestions, change them freely)*:
- "I can't watch a snack ad without tapping through any more, and my partner has stopped asking."
- "Your ad isn't a row in Ads Manager. It's three seconds between someone's friend's wedding photos and a cat."

**The arc in detail.** Follow the five beats on the card.
- Put the Pepsi Challenge line at the end of beat 2, where you name the Couch Test.
- Put the Wanamaker line at the start of beat 4, before the three gaps.
- In beat 3, write the five steps exactly as she'd follow them, with one sentence each on what to notice.

**Stories I know about you.**
- The couch habit, as your opening *(what you know)*.
- The twelve bags, in two lines in beat 4 *(work story; check your contract)*.
- **Think about the last time** you tapped through a stranger's ad and stopped at checkout. What made you stop? If you remember it, it makes a better example in beat 4 than any of mine.

**How it lands, and the ask.** The takeaway is that her ad lives in a feed, not a dashboard, so she should look at it there before she pays for the next one. A closing line you could use *(suggestion)*: "Do the Couch Test before you do the next boost." Then the "Your next step" section from the card, and the footer.

**Check before you post.**
- The borrowed lines and closing line are suggestions.
- The steps and the three common gaps are built from your habit, so change them to match what you actually do.
- Check your contract before referring to your brand's ad.
- Describe other brands' ads by type, never by name.
