# The Offer Builder: clear packages and what to charge

Turn their chosen offer into packages someone can say yes to.

The five tests every package must pass:
1. The transformation: from X to Y in a set window. An "audit" is a deliverable; the transformation is what changes after it.
2. One mechanism: how it's delivered (embedded fractional, fixed project, productized service, coached package, group cohort, product). Pick one per package. Mixing means freelancing.
3. A price band: low and high, plus one line on what justifies the top.
4. Who it's for and who it isn't: a specific buyer, situation and moment, and the buyer they'd turn down.
5. An expansion path: project to retainer, one-to-one to group, audit credited against the main project.

Shape the packages as a ladder, not a menu of tiers to choose from:
- a front door: the smallest sellable piece of the main offer, close to the buyer's problem, its fee credited toward the main package. A front door is an entry point, never the whole business.
- the core package: the main offer, with what's included, a scope line (what's in and what's out, with numbers), and what the client holds at the end, which must be worth buying even if they never hire them again.
- the ongoing option: what happens after (a retainer, an advisory month, a second phase).

Pricing, from the buyer, checked against their numbers:
- Start from the buyer: what the outcome is worth to them and what they already pay for similar help. Unless you've been given researched prices with sources, call the band a hypothesis and give its basis in one sentence. Similar businesses or published prices are signals, never proof that this buyer will pay.
- Then the hours-to-money check: their monthly goal divided by realistic monthly hours (hours per week x 4.33) shows what an hour of their time needs to earn for the offer to fit their goal. It tells them whether the offer fits; it never sets what the buyer pays. If it doesn't fit, say so and suggest moving a dial (scope, format, how many at once, this season's goal) rather than raising the price to match the goal.
- Count all the time: preparation, delivery, finding buyers and admin. Count meetings at 2 to 2.5 times their length. Count costs too (venue, materials, ingredients, platform fees), and keep revenue, profit and profit per working hour apart.
- Capacity check: clients needed = monthly goal / price; hours needed = clients x hours each. If it doesn't fit their week, move one dial: price, mechanism or this season's goal.
- No unlimited anything. Take payment before work starts. A payment plan beats a discount.
- Always label prices "a starting point to test". Never promise what anyone will earn.

Also give an anxiety-reducer suited to the buyer (a fixed scope, a check-in point, a fee credit), and the first test: one way to put the front door in front of a real buyer this week.
