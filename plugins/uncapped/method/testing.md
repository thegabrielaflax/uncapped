# Testing a direction with real buyers

Every offer is a hypothesis, in pencil, until buyers confirm it. Packaging, pricing or launching it doesn't establish that anyone needs it or will pay. Say so plainly and keep it visible.

## Assumptions and the most important uncertainty
- Write the hypothesis in two parts: the problem, and who has it.
- List the assumptions in three columns: the assumption; whether a buyer has actually said it (only what the person reports a buyer said counts, never anything Claude wrote); or whether it is the person's own thought. Test the own-thoughts first, especially about pain and money, then the buyer, then format and price.
- Validate the problem separately from the format. Don't spend time proving what is already true.
- Name the buying moments and who can approve the spend. Prefer buyers who already feel the pain.
- Flag credibility risk: whether buyers will accept help from someone like this person can be the biggest risk of all.
- Every conversation has three must-learns, and one is always about money.
- Gap (say so if relevant): Pivot School's materials give no formal way to rank assumptions by risk beyond "test your own thoughts first".

## Choosing the experiment
Match the experiment to the uncertainty (this matching is Uncapped's reading of the method, not a rule Gabriela wrote down):
- Problem unclear: problem conversations, plus desk research while calls are slow.
- Buyer unclear: two or three buyer groups, three or four conversations each.
- Interest shown but price untested: let them name a number, then offer a scoped paid pilot.
- Unsure they'd enjoy or can deliver the work: a small beta (four people, the fifth pays full rate).
- Framing unclear: say it out loud to buyers and watch for the follow-up question.
- Cold network: a content or credibility experiment first, on a two-month horizon rather than a week.
Keep it small, cheap and time-boxed, alongside conversations, never instead of them. One direction, one buyer, a decision date and a literal first message to send this week. No more than two or three hypotheses at once. Fit it to their hours (side work rarely more than about ten a week while employed) and their energy (in a heavy season, one conversation or one tightly scoped project). Start with people who would reply to a text from them; a warm introduction beats a perfect profile.

## Research conversations, not sales calls
Research conversations are a different job from selling. Never pitch, coach or price on one; deflect advice requests and price questions. If a clear problem surfaces, a scoped paid pilot can be offered, and research continues alongside.
- Outreach: short, specific, in the buyer's words, asking for their expertise. How you know each other, what you're exploring and why them, a no-pitch line and a time. Never "pick your brain". Send to five first and adjust if there's silence. About ten messages in a week; a pattern needs about ten conversations.
- Guide shape for 20 to 30 minutes: two warm-ups, four or five past-behaviour questions about the problem, two money questions in the middle, two closers. The questions never mention the idea.
- Before a guide goes out, check every question for leading wording, an assumption built in or a hypothetical ("would you pay for…"). Keep the pitch out of a learning conversation.
- Ask about the past, never the future, then dig into the answer. Name the topic in each question. Follow an unexpected answer rather than working the list.
- Money every time: what they spend now (time or money), what they've paid for before to fix it, what doing nothing costs, who else they considered, and who approved it.
- Close: thanks, permission to follow up, two specific introductions, and "Is there anything I should have asked you and didn't?"
- Useful wording: "Walk me through the last time [problem] came up. What did you do?" · "When did it last actually cost you something: time, money or stress?" · "What have you paid for before that was meant to fix it?" · "If nothing changed here for a year, what's going to happen?" · "You will not hurt my feelings either way."
- Never ask: "Would you pay for this?", "Would you use this?", "Do you like my idea?", "Would it be helpful if...?"
- Capture each conversation: who, their exact words, what they did (behavior), what it cost them, how they buy, and the person's own conviction afterwards.

## Reading the evidence
- Strong signal is specific, in the past and costs the buyer something (time, reputation, money). Weak signal is vague, in the future and free.
- "That sounds great" and "I'd probably buy that" are reactions, not evidence. Questions about cost, start date or who else is involved are real signal.
- Silence is ambiguous: targeting, message, timing or the offer. Many replies come on the second or third message; follow up with something useful, never "just checking in". A week with no replies: lean on warmer contacts.
- Not evidence: compliments, likes, people enjoying their writing, a gatekeeper's opinion about budget, their own problem.
- Two conversations conclude nothing. Don't sort patterns before five; a pattern needs around ten. Don't rewrite the script after one call.
- Keep evidence levels separate: a problem exists; someone will pay; it can be delivered profitably (check the effective hourly rate against their goal and hours); sales are repeatable (consistent sales over months). Gabriela's materials don't set thresholds for these; say what the evidence supports and its limits, never "validated" as a badge.

## Deciding what next
Name what happened before changing anything: the offer holds (build and price it); good offer, wrong buyer (move the buyer); wrong offer (reshape around what buyers kept coming back to); right offer, wrong framing (change the words, keep the substance); too early to tell (keep testing). Try the smallest change first: a tighter buyer, their words, then reshaping. The trap is rebuilding when re-aiming would do. "I don't want this buyer" is a valid result. Pausing means turning a direction down to a low flame, not deleting it. Every test has a review date; close research periods rather than letting them run for months.

## Paid pilots
Reflect what came up in their words, propose a scoped test, state the deliverable and the price. Real money, priced from the buyer, the outcome and the scope (not a token amount, and not inflated to look serious); a pilot price rather than a discount based on assumptions; one window, with exclusions listed. A pilot buys proof, a case study and introductions, not profit. Gap: Pivot School's notes say little about pilot terms or turning a pilot client into a full-price client; say so if asked.

## Desk research
Look at 5 to 8 comparable services: who they serve, what changes for the client, packages, price (or "not published"), proof, where they show up, what they don't touch. Where prices cluster is the band; who nobody serves well is the gap. Competition is evidence that people spend; never price to the cheapest. A provider existing, its followers or its engagement don't prove it sells. Desk research never answers what only buyers can; one conversation can beat a week of research. Link every source; separate what the page says from what you infer; say when a site couldn't be read (LinkedIn and Instagram often need a login).
