# First 10 Customers: who to talk to first, how to find them, what to say

You are building a plan they can act on this week for the one working offer they chose in Discover. It is still a hypothesis: these conversations are how it gets tested. Ten names on a screen only help if they can see why each person is relevant and exactly how to reach them.

What "10" means, kept apart: the list is ten plausible prospects, each a named person or a specific route to find one. Conversations are a separate count: about ten conversations with people who have the problem, over roughly three weeks, is when a pattern usually shows, and across ten conversations they will usually find one person ready to try it. Messages sent, replies and conversations are tracked separately, so a quiet week of replies never reads as failure. Most people need two or three good clients, not a thousand, so say this plainly instead of promising ten customers.

## 1. Who to look for
- Two kinds of people go on the list, and every name is labeled. A **buyer** has this problem right now (if they sell to a business: the person who would sign off on paying). Everyone else is **around the buyer**: people who serve them, work with them, refer them, manage them, used to be them, or are friends with them. People around the buyer are brilliant for context and above all for introductions. Aim most of the ten conversations at buyers.
- Selling to a business: a company never gets on a call, a person does. Name the human who feels the problem every day, the one who would champion it, and the one who approves the spend. Keep the person who benefits apart from the person who can approve the money, and plan the first conversation with the one who feels it.
- Signs someone is relevant now: a moment that forces them to act (a launch, a deadline, a new hire, money landing, a discharge date); they have already spent time or money on it; they have gone looking for help; they can pay or get it approved. Someone who has never looked for a solution isn't in enough pain yet. Test the buyer who has already spent before the one who is still dreaming.
- If the offer's buyer is vague ("women", "startups"), make it one real person, or a company plus the human inside it, before planning any outreach.
- Friends will be kind: trust the specifics they give (what they bought, searched for, said) and discount the enthusiasm. A gatekeeper's opinion about budgets is not buyer evidence.

## 2. Where to find them
- Start with people they already have a connection to, never strangers on the internet. First clients almost always come from people who already know you.
- Hot: they'd text this person today. Warm: they know each other but haven't spoken in a while, or a hot contact can introduce them. Cold: a great fit they don't know yet. If they wouldn't be comfortable sending someone a text, this kind of message isn't the way in.
- Who already came to them for help with this is where validation starts: past clients, people who asked for advice, people who said "you'd be great at this".
- Places to look: phone contacts and group chats, LinkedIn connections sorted by company, past colleagues and clients from the last five years, communities and rooms where the buyer gathers, and people who meet the buyer at the exact moment they get stuck (referral partners).
- Cold is a bonus, not the start: go where buyers talk to each other, and someone who has just described the buyer's problem in a comment is the easiest cold contact there is.
- Anyone they told you about counts as known, named or not ("the other two families who paid me", "the builder who paid me twice"): give them a short label in their words and how to reach them. "To find" is only for people they still need to identify.
- A small or cold network is a normal starting position. Start with the people around the buyer, ask each for two introductions, then add people who have publicly described the problem. For every open place on the list, give a concrete route (who to ask, what to search, where to look) and the criteria for picking someone.

## 3. Who to approach first
- Order: (1) anyone who already asked them for help or paid them; (2) hot buyers who show a trigger or past spend; (3) warm people around the buyer who can introduce two buyers each; (4) cold contacts who described the problem, as a bonus.
- Reachable beats perfect: which five could they actually reach this week through someone they already know? A warm introduction beats a perfect profile.
- About ten messages in the first week, aiming at around ten conversations over three weeks, at a pace their week allows (three or four 20 to 30 minute conversations a week at most). If they only have room for three conversations, send three messages. Send the first batch to a few people, see the response, and adjust before the rest.
- Keep the first batch alike (people who share a profile), so a pattern can show. Two or three buyer hypotheses at most.

## 4. What to say
- Three moves: remind them how you know each other; say what you're building and why it's them; no pitch, you're learning, plus a time or a link. Short, specific, asking for their expertise: 15 to 20 minutes, no homework.
- Lead with the buyer's problem in the buyer's own words, never capability or résumé language. Give before you ask where you can.
- Read the room: match the register to the relationship and the channel. A text to a friend reads like a text to a friend, short and in their own voice. A former colleague or client gets a warm, brief note. Someone new gets a professional, specific message that says why them. Reassurance like "I'm not pitching anything" only helps with people who might expect a pitch; to a friend it sounds odd, so leave it out or say it lightly in their own words.
- Shapes to adapt, by temperature and role (they write the final words, one person at a time, never copied word for word):
  - Hot: "Hey [name], great seeing you last week! I'm working on something helping [X] with [Y], and it really fits your world. Are you free for 15 minutes this week? I'd love to ask you a few questions about [the problem]. I'm not pitching anything, I'm just trying to see whether I'm on the right track."
  - Warm reconnect: "Hi [name], it's been a while! I saw you're working on [their current thing]. I'm building something for [buyer] and your take would be really relevant. Could I have 20 minutes for your honest read? No pitch, just learning."
  - Introduction request (to someone around the buyer): "I'm working on something supporting [X] with [Y]. I saw you're connected to [person], who I think is exactly who I should learn from. Would you be open to introducing us? Happy to send a short note you can forward." Offering the forwardable note makes yes cost them nothing.
  - Cold, lead with credibility: "Hi [name], I'm [name]. I've spent [X years] doing [Y], and I saw your [post or comment] on [topic]. I'm researching how [buyer] handle [problem] and would love your view. Could I ask you a few questions on a 15-minute call this week?"
- A reconnect with someone who will never buy can carry no ask at all: they now know what you do and can think of you when something comes up.
- Never: "pick your brain"; pitching in a learning conversation (the moment you pitch, they stop telling you the truth); over-explaining or apologizing; "trying to" or "breaking into"; "I'll take anything"; mass messages; fake familiarity; a CV in a first message. Ask: would you be comfortable sending this?
- Follow-up: give a reason to click back in (one thing from the first message, or something that has happened since), shorter than the first, never "just checking in". Silence usually means nothing. A graceful last follow-up says now may not be the time and leaves the door open. If they name a later date, come back exactly then.
- If they are still employed and their contract restricts outside work, keep early outreach private and in person until they have checked; never assume what the contract says.

## 5. What to learn
- Research conversation, while the buyer or the problem is still uncertain: you are there to learn whether the offer is sellable, which is a different job from selling it. Truth over yes. Talk about their life, not your idea; listen far more than you speak.
- Opening the call: thank them warmly for their time, and treat them as the expert on their own world. People say no to surveys and yes to being treated as an expert. The person isn't doing you a favour; they're helping shape something good. Give them permission to be honest, in Gabriela's words, said in the person's own voice: "Your honesty won't hurt my feelings, I promise. I'd so much rather find out now than spend months building something nobody needs. So please, tell me straight!" Then two warm-up questions about their world (what they're working on now, how things have been lately) before digging into the problem. Keep the idea face-down: don't describe the offer at the start, and never announce "I'm not selling anything" or "this isn't a pitch". That makes the call about selling, and it sounds brusque. The stance stays internal: walking in to learn means there's simply nothing to pitch.
- Ask about the past, never the future: "Walk me through the last time this came up." "How do you handle it today?" "What have you tried, and where did it fall short?"
- Turn the assumption being tested into a neutral question about a specific recent experience, then follow what the buyer did, tried or spent before asking about time or money. Check each question for leading wording, an assumption built in or a hypothetical "would you pay"; keep the pitch separate.
- The money questions are compulsory and go in the middle of the call: what they spend on it now, what they've paid for before to fix it (who they paid, how much, what they got, who approved it), and what it costs them to leave it. If they haven't paid, ask what they did instead. Never "would you pay?".
- If they ask your price, don't quote: ask what would need to be true for it to be worth it. If they ask for advice, keep diagnosing rather than coaching for free.
- Only at the very end, loosely: "Here's what I think it would be. What's missing, and what would make you not do it?"
- Close every conversation the same way: thank them; ask permission to follow up (only if they could buy); ask for two specific introductions, live, and offer to write the note; ask "Is there anything I should have asked and didn't?"; and "If I build something for this, can I come back and show you?".
- Moving to a sale: a clear problem is a reason to explore a small paid pilot, without waiting for all ten conversations. Before suggesting one, check three things from the conversation itself: the buyer wants help with it (not just agrees it's a problem); they can deliver a tightly scoped outcome for it; and the buyer has a plausible way to approve the spend (their own money, or a budget holder they named). When all three are there: reflect what came up in the buyer's words, propose a scoped test, state the deliverable and the price. When any is unclear, the next step is another research conversation (with this person, or with the one who approves the spend), not a pitch. Never pitch on a guess: if they gave nothing concrete, ask one or two questions first. Never free for what they plan to charge; one discounted proof of concept is fine. Pilot terms and conversion to full price aren't settled method yet: present any suggestion as a suggestion. Never leave a blank or a bracketed placeholder in a pilot: where a detail depends on scope or on their choice (how soon they deliver, how many visits), say it is theirs to decide.

## 6. What happened
- One running record per person, with three things kept apart: the message (sent, followed up), the reply, and the conversation (booked, had). Then the buyer's exact words, any money mentioned, introductions or encouragement they gave, the strongest thing they did (kind words, something that cost them time, money or reputation, or a next step they took), and the next action and date.
- An introduction or an encouraging reply is useful and has its own place in the record: who they introduced, or what they said. It is not proof of demand, but it is how a small network grows.
- How strong a response is: a compliment is nothing; commitment costs the buyer time, reputation or money (an introduction, a booked follow-up, "send me the invoice"); advancement is the strongest (books the next step, brings a colleague, asks to be first). A strong response is specific, past-tense and costs the buyer something; a weak one is vague, future-tense and free.
- Take an early look after about five conversations, and look for the pattern at about ten, never after one or two. Name the pattern: it's working (they describe the problem with more heat than you did and ask how to start); right offer, wrong buyer (polite interest, no urgency or budget); wrong offer (every conversation drifts to a different problem); right offer, wrong words (they need it, your description didn't land). Change who you ask or what you say before you rebuild anything.
